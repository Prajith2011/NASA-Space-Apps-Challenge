2 · Inspecting parts & their risks

🖱️ Look around

Drag to rotate, scroll / pinch to zoom. Hover any part: it lights up with a glowing outline and shows its name. ⟲ Reset view brings the camera back.

🧩 Parts & links list

The side panel lists every part (15–20 per spacecraft) and every connection: the cables, joints, hinges and pipes that join two parts. Use the search box, and hover a row to light it up in 3D.

📋 Click for details

Clicking a part or connection (in 3D or in the list) opens a card with an overview, its known vulnerabilities, and the connections it has. Many vulnerabilities really happened, marked (real).

💥 Exploded view

Every part slides outward so you can see what is inside. Press 🧲 Put it back together to reassemble it.

3 · Diagnose: finding the dangers

Press 🩺 DIAGNOSE. A holographic scan sweeps over the spacecraft and checks every part and every connection, one by one, in a live feed:

  • ✓ SAFE lines turn green in the feed. Safe parts are left alone in 3D.
  • ✗ DANGER lines turn red. The part glows red with a pulsing outline, a shock-wave ring pops out of it, and dangerous connections appear as glowing red links between the two parts.

🎲 Every spacecraft always hides at least 5 dangers (exactly 5 on Beginner, 5–7 on Intermediate, 7–9 on Advanced), and they are different every time you open it. Running the scan again simulates another year in space with new problems.

7 · Why it's like a Rubik's cube

On a Rubik's cube, twisting one face to fix it scrambles the faces next to it. Spacecraft are the same, because every part affects several things at once:

  • ⚡ A bigger solar panel makes more power… but adds mass.
  • 🚀 A stronger rocket can lift more… but costs more budget.
  • 🔬 Better instruments do more science… but use more power.

So a move can fix one checklist answer and break another. When that happens, the game tells you exactly what broke and why. That's not a mistake, it's the puzzle! Real engineers call this a trade-off.

8 · The space ideas, in plain English

These are the things the checklist asks about. You don't need to memorise them: the game explains each one when it matters.

🏋️ Mass & the launch rocket

Every rocket can only lift a certain mass. If the spacecraft is too heavy, you must make it lighter or buy a bigger (more expensive) rocket. Like a lift with a weight limit.

⚡ Power (watts)

Solar panels or a nuclear generator make power, and instruments, heaters and radios use it. You need to make at least as much as you use. On Mars, dust storms block sunlight, so solar panels make much less.

📡 Science & data

Instruments collect science, but it only counts once the data reaches Earth. A weak radio can't send everything home. Like taking 1,000 photos with a slow internet connection.

📦 Fitting in the nose cone

The spacecraft rides inside the rocket's nose cone (the "fairing"). Anything wider than the nose cone must fold up for launch, like the Webb telescope's mirror.

❄️ Cold infrared cameras

Infrared light is heat. A warm telescope "glows" and hides faint galaxies, so infrared cameras must be extremely cold, using a sunshield or coolers.

💰 Budget

Every part costs money (in $ millions), and the mission has a cost cap. Stronger rockets and fancy parts eat the budget fast.

🚀 Δv ("delta-v"): the fuel power to change speed. Try it!

Δv is how much a spacecraft can change its speed using its fuel. To land on the Moon, the lander needs enough Δv to slow down from orbit. The rocket equation says Δv = efficiency × 9.8 × ln(full mass ÷ empty mass). Move the sliders and see the surprise: making the lander lighter helps more than you'd think, because the fuel has less to push.

9 · Walkthrough: fixing the Mars rover

Here is a complete solve of the first mission, with the game's real numbers. Try it yourself first if you'd like a spoiler-free game!

THE STARTING DESIGN (BROKEN)

Atlas V 541 rocket, standard chassis, large solar wings, large battery, full chemistry lab, relay radio.

  • ✗ Light enough for the rocket? The spacecraft weighs 3,750 kg, but the rocket lifts only 3,700 kg.
  • ✗ Enough power in a dust storm? The solar wings drop to 90 W, plus 60 W of battery reserve = 150 W, but the rover needs 180 W.
  • ✓ Science returned   ✓ Budget ($1,885M of $1,900M)

A TEMPTING TRAP (DON'T DO THIS)

"Too heavy? Just buy a bigger rocket!" Swapping to the Delta IV Heavy fixes the mass… but the mission now costs $2,065M, over the $1,900M budget. One fix, one new problem. Classic Rubik's cube!

MOVE 1: POWER SOURCE → NUCLEAR GENERATOR (MMRTG)

Dust can't block a nuclear generator, and it's lighter than the solar wings (45 kg vs 90 kg).

  • ✗ Mass: now 3,705 kg, so still 5 kg too heavy!
  • ✗ Power: 110 W + 60 W battery = 170 W, still short of the 180 W needed.
  • ✗ Budget: the generator costs more, so the total is now $1,955M (over $1,900M).

Looks worse… but now the real culprit is easy to spot: the power-hungry, heavy, expensive chemistry lab.

MOVE 2: SCIENCE SUITE → CAMERAS, LASER SPECTROMETER & DRILL

It's lighter (75 kg vs 120 kg), uses less power (80 W vs 110 W) and costs less ($200M vs $350M), but it still meets the science goal.

  • ✓ Mass: 3,660 kg ≤ 3,700 kg
  • ✓ Power: 170 W ≥ 150 W needed
  • ✓ Science: 6 points ≥ 6 goal
  • ✓ Budget: $1,805M ≤ $1,900M

🎉 Solved in 2 moves, which matches par for ⭐⭐⭐!

OTHER WAYS TO WIN

You could also keep the solar wings and switch to the lightweight chassis + the same science suite. That's 2 moves too! There's rarely one perfect answer in engineering. At the end, the game shows what NASA chose for Curiosity (the nuclear generator) and why.

10 · Launch & the 3D finale

When the checklist is all YES, press 🚀 LAUNCH THE MISSION! You watch your rocket lift off in a short movie filmed from six cameras (wide shot of the pad at dusk, a close-up of the engines lighting, a camera on the tower, a long-lens tracking shot through the clouds, a camera on the rocket looking down at Earth, and a chase camera in space). You'll see booster separation, stage separation and the nose cone (fairing) opening to reveal your spacecraft. Then comes the journey: Mars and Moon missions first "park" in orbit around Earth, then fire again to head off. A spacecraft going to Mars escapes Earth completely and orbits the Sun for about 8 months until it meets Mars. Live altitude and speed are shown; press "Skip launch" to jump ahead), then you play the mission with the spacecraft you designed. Your design choices really matter here.

13 · Tips & questions

Where do I start?

Read the first ✗ NO in the checklist and click one of its "Parts to try" buttons. Keep Easy mode on, and each option will tell you what it fixes or breaks.

My move broke something. Did I mess up?

No, that's the puzzle! Read the red message: it says exactly which number went wrong. Often one more swap fixes everything (see the walkthrough above).

Look for the "culprit" part

If one part appears in several ✗ answers (for example, a heavy, power-hungry instrument), swapping just that part can fix many problems at once.

Is there only one right answer?

No. Every mission has several valid designs. The reveal screen tells you how many and compares yours with NASA's real choice.

Are the numbers real?

The part numbers are simplified so the puzzle works. The facts on the "What NASA Built" screen are real and link to NASA sources.

Why are the dangers different every time?

Real spacecraft never fail the same way twice. Each time you open a spacecraft, the game picks at least 5 problems at random: some based on real events, others from that part's known weak points, with fresh sensor readings.

Are the diagnostic readings real?

The readings are simulated for the game. The parts, what they do, and the vulnerabilities marked "real" come from the actual missions (each spacecraft links to its NASA page).

The 3D looks slow on my computer

The 3D views automatically lower their resolution if the frame rate drops. Closing other tabs helps too.

The 3D view is blank

The 3D engine loads from the internet, so check your connection and refresh. You can still play with the parts list.
